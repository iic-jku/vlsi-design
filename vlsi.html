1 Introduction

This is the material for an introductory-level course on digital very-large-scale integration (VLSI) design, held at JKU under course number 336.051 (“KV VLSI Design”).

The course teaches the design of digital application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) with a modern, fully open-source RTL-to-GDSII flow. We model hardware in SystemVerilog, verify it with cocotb (Python), inspect waveforms in GTKWave/Surfer, and harden, assemble, and tape out a complete chip, including the padframe, with LibreLane on the IHP SG13CMOS5L 130 nm open PDK. In the course project, you take your own RISC-V SoC all the way to a tapeout-ready chip.

1.1 Preamble on the Use of AI

Artificial intelligence offers impressive possibilities today: it can explain how things are connected, provide examples, help with debugging, or point out different ways to solve a problem. Precisely for this reason, however, there is a risk of relying too heavily on such tools while learning, and thereby not practicing enough of exactly those skills that this course is meant to build.

In the written exam, you will have to solve problems without any technical aids. There, the only thing that counts is which concepts, methods, and skills you have mastered yourself. Likewise, in the oral presentation of your project, you have to be able to explain what you did and why you did it.

If you want to complete a long-distance run in a few months, you have to build up your own endurance. You could, of course, cover your weekly training route faster on an e-scooter, but that does not train your ability to run the distance yourself. The same holds for your studies: if AI regularly does the actual thinking for you, you will not sufficiently build up the skills you need.

So feel free to use AI as a learning partner, but not as a substitute for your own thinking. Always try to understand and solve a problem on your own first. Afterwards, use AI, for example, to check your solution, to learn about an alternative approach, or to have a difficult topic explained to you. The goal of this course is not to arrive at a correct answer as quickly as possible, but to develop the competence to get there on your own.

In the end, you are here to learn something, so do not cheat yourself! This matters beyond the course, too: as AI agents take over more and more routine design work (see Section 1.11), engineers who know how to prompt these tools and, above all, how to critically review their results will always be needed. And you can only review what you understand yourself.

1.2 Learning Objectives

After completing this course, you will be able to:

  • work with the IIC-OSIC-TOOLS container and manage your designs with Git and GitHub;
  • read and write synthesizable SystemVerilog RTL for synchronous digital designs, and translate idiomatic VHDL into idiomatic SystemVerilog;
  • apply modeling guidelines that lead to clean, fast, and testable synthesis results (avoiding latches, inferring proper registers, structuring arithmetic);
  • verify RTL with self-checking cocotb testbenches and reason about coverage and corner cases;
  • understand and explain the RTL-to-GDSII flow (synthesis, floorplanning, placement, clock tree synthesis, routing, signoff) and drive it with LibreLane;
  • analyze timing using the concepts of setup/hold, propagation delay, clock skew, and static timing analysis (STA);
  • explain design for test (DFT), the stuck-at fault model, scan chains, and test coverage;
  • handle multiple clock domains safely with synchronizers and reason about metastability;
  • integrate a digital macro into a padframe and assemble a complete, tapeout-ready ASIC with open-source tools;
  • design, verify, harden, and document a RISC-V SoC with your own extension as a capstone project;
  • optionally, use LLM agents efficiently, and critically, in a chip design workflow.

1.6 Why ASICs?

Application-specific integrated circuits are designed when standard, off-the-shelf components cannot meet the requirements. Typical motivations are:

  • Specification: performance, power, or size targets unreachable with standard parts.
  • Integration: combining digital logic, analog circuits, and on-chip memory into a single mixed-signal chip.
  • Economics: at high volume the per-unit cost drops dramatically, and integrating more functionality into one chip reduces system cost.

Historically, the cost per transistor has fallen by orders of magnitude as integration density doubled roughly every 18–24 months (Moore’s law). What was once only affordable to large companies is, with open-source PDKs and tools, now accessible to students and researchers.

1.8 The Digital Design Flow

The core of this course is the digital design flow that turns a hardware description into a manufacturable layout. Since this layout is traditionally handed over to the foundry in the GDSII file format, it is called the RTL-to-GDSII (or simply RTL-to-GDS) flow. Figure 1 shows its main steps.

The flow starts with the RTL design, described in a hardware description language (HDL) such as SystemVerilog. Logic synthesis translates the RTL into a gate-level netlist of standard cells of the target technology, guided by constraints such as the clock frequency. Long before any layout exists, the design is simulated extensively: first at RTL, and then as a synthesized netlist (pre-layout simulation). Every bug found here leads back to the HDL.

The physical implementation then turns the netlist into a layout. Floorplanning defines the size of the die and the core, the placement of I/Os and macros, and the power grid. Placement positions the standard cells, clock tree synthesis (CTS) builds a balanced clock distribution network, and routing connects all cells with metal wires.

Only after routing are the resistances and capacitances of the wires known. Parasitic extraction annotates them to the netlist, on which static timing analysis (STA) and post-layout simulation check whether the design still meets its timing and functional specification. Violations are fixed by revisiting floorplanning and placement or, if this is not enough, by changing the RTL itself (design iteration), e.g., by pipelining a critical path. Finally, physical verification checks the layout against the design rules of the foundry (design rule check, DRC) and against the netlist (layout versus schematic, LVS). Only a design that passes all checks goes to tape-out, i.e., is sent to the foundry for fabrication.

Besides these steps, design for test inserts scan chains so that every fabricated chip can be tested (Section 8), and designs with several clocks need safe clock domain crossings (Section 9). For a complete chip, the hardened digital macro is finally integrated into a padframe and assembled into the chip top level (Section 10). 1.10 The AMS Chip Template

Hardening a single digital block is only part of the way to a chip. A tapeout-ready chip additionally needs a padframe and a seal ring, the placement and wiring of all macros on the top level, logo and metal-fill insertion, full-chip verification, and finally a bonding diagram for packaging. To get there without fighting the tools, this course builds on the ihp-sg13cmos5l-ams-chip-template (Dorrer and Pretl 2026), an open-source GitHub template repository for analog mixed-signal (AMS) chips developed at JKU:

  • It is released under the permissive Solderpad Hardware License v2.1.
  • Create your own repository with GitHub’s Use this template button (or simply git clone it), enter the IIC-OSIC-TOOLS container, and run make all. The result is a tapeout-ready AMS chip.
  • A step-by-step tutorial, written in Quarto, walks through all IC design steps on IHP’s 130 nm process, with simple exercises to learn by doing. You start your project with this tutorial (Section 11).
  • Regression tests via GitHub Actions ensure that the flow keeps working with every new release of the tools.

The template is also available for the ihp-sg13g2, sky130A, and gf180mcuD open PDKs, and several chips have already been taped out with it, including TinyWhisper (Section 1.12).

1.10.1 Flow Overview

The Makefile-driven flow of the template covers:

  • simulation, linting, and hardening of the RTL;
  • layout checks and reports of the hardened digital macro;
  • simulation of all analog testbenches, including process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) corners and Monte Carlo (MC) analysis;
  • layout checks of the analog and digital blocks (DRC, LVS, and parasitic extraction, PEX);
  • digital-on-top assembly of the chip top level and its layout check (DRC), including
    • seal ring and padframe generation,
    • macro placement and wiring, also with non-default rules (NDRs), and
    • logo and fill insertion;
  • full top-level LVS and simulation without black-boxing;
  • generation of the bonding diagram.

1.10.2 Folder Structure and Makefiles

The folder structure of the template was discussed with the FOSSi community. Every macro in macros/ (e.g., the digital counter and the analog inverter examples) follows the same scheme as the chip top level, with its own Makefile and folders such as rtl/, testbenches/, schematic/, layout/, netlist/, verification/, and final/. Since a macro can itself contain macros, the structure is recursive. This makes it easy to share macros between projects and to reproduce the results of others.

At the top level, make all runs four steps:

  1. make build-all builds the bondpad, the logos, all enabled macros, and finally the chip top level (LibreLane assembly, reports, GDS, netlists, logo and fill insertion, and renders);
  2. make magic-drc (or make klayout-drc) checks the final layout of the chip;
  3. make sim-all simulates the chip (RTL and gate-level simulation with cocotb, gate-level simulation with Xschem);
  4. make bondplan generates the bonding diagram for packaging.

Targets that build an IP block or a macro recursively call the Makefile of that block.

1.11 Agentic AI Chip Design

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly able to operate a complete chip design environment: they do not only answer questions, but also edit design files, run tools, interpret the results, and iterate. Figure 2 shows such an agentic workflow.

The designer interacts with the LLM agent through prompts and reviews. The agent edits the RTL, schematics, and layout of a chip project based on the AMS chip template (Section 1.10) and runs its make targets, which call the tools of the IIC-OSIC-TOOLS. The results of simulation, DRC, LVS, and PEX flow back to the agent and to the designer. A knowledge base (an index plus notes) lets the agent retrieve relevant information and distill new insights for later sessions. All changes are tracked in version control (e.g., Git and GitHub): the agent creates branches, commits and pushes changes, sets up CI workflows, and opens pull requests, while the designer reviews the diffs and decides whether changes are pushed or discarded. Of course, the designer can still edit the project manually at any time.

Such a workflow is only possible because every step of the flow is scripted, every tool runs in a reproducible container, and every result is machine-readable. Note the role of the designer: they remain responsible for the design and have to judge whether the results of the agent are correct, which brings us back to Section 1.1. Using LLM agents efficiently is an optional learning objective of this course.

1.12 Example: The TinyWhisper SoC

What is possible with open-source tools and the AMS chip template is demonstrated by TinyWhisper, an open-source, fully integrated, multi-mode short-wave transmitter for amateur radio, developed in a cooperation between JMU Würzburg and JKU Linz. TinyWhisper combines a RISC-V CPU with peripherals, custom digital signal generation, and an analog front-end on one chip, so it is a good preview of what you will build in the course project. The block diagram and all design files can be found in the repository, and the full documentation is available online.

1.12.1 Weak Signal Propagation Reporter

TinyWhisper targets the Weak Signal Propagation Reporter (WSPR) protocol, a popular digital mode in amateur (ham) radio for propagation studies in the 80 m to 10 m bands. A WSPR transmitter sends a short message (callsign, location, and transmit power) using 4-FSK modulation with a tone spacing of \(\Delta f = 1.46\,\mathrm{Hz}\) and a symbol duration of \(T = 0.683\,\mathrm{s}\). Receivers can decode signals down to \(-31\,\mathrm{dB}\) below the noise floor in a 2500 Hz bandwidth and report everything they receive to a central database via the Internet (WSPRnet map). On TinyWhisper, the modem part (coding, framing, and symbol mapping) is implemented in firmware written in C, which runs on the on-chip RISC-V CPU.

1.12.2 Chip Overview

Table 4 summarizes the key parameters of the chip, and Figure 3 shows renders of its layout. The custom logo on the topmost metal layer (Figure 3 (b)) was generated with the tool ArtistIC of the PULP platform. TinyWhisper was taped out in the IHP open MPW run in March 2026 and, at the time of writing (October 2026), is still in fabrication.

The digital core is built around an embedded RISC-V CPU implementing rv32e_zicsr, i.e., the 32-bit embedded base integer instruction set with 16 registers plus the extension for control and status registers. The CPU is a multi-cycle design without pipelining, running at 56 MHz, and accesses its SPI, I²C, UART, and GPIO peripherals as memory-mapped I/O. The I/Q baseband signals are generated in hardware: a 30-bit iterative CORDIC computes sine and cosine with a frequency resolution of \(\Delta f = f_\mathrm{clk}/2^{30} \approx 0.05\,\mathrm{Hz}\), and a single-bit second-order delta-sigma modulator (oversampling ratio up to 256) converts its output into a bitstream for the analog front-end. Both are configurable via software. Furthermore, the digital core divides the clock down to the 25% duty-cycle local oscillator (LO) signals of the mixers, which cover the 80 m, 40 m, 20 m, and 10 m bands.

The RISC-V CPU has already been proven in silicon: it was part of a multi-project student chip (with digital filters, mini games, and more) fabricated in the GlobalFoundries GF180MCU technology in the first wafer.space run (GitHub). On a breadboard, with the firmware supplied by an external microcontroller board and a discrete third-order multiple-feedback low-pass filter, the chip generates a clean baseband signal.

The analog front-end is an IQ modulator. Each of its two channels consists of a third-order multiple-feedback (MFB) Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 400 kHz, built around a Barthélemy/Manfredini inverter-based operational transconductance amplifier (OTA), followed by a passive CMOS voltage-mode mixer with simple transmission-gate (TG) switches and a single-to-differential conversion circuit. Remarkably, the front-end consists only of inverter- and TG-based circuits. As an IQ transmitter, it supports not only WSPR but also other modes such as FT8, SSB, and CW.

Finally, even the bonding diagram for packaging is generated automatically: a Python script reads a configuration file with the pinout and placement, extracts the relevant layers (top metal and pads) from the layout, places the chip in the package, determines the pad centers, draws the bond wires, and renders the result as GDS. This is exactly what make bondplan of the AMS chip template does (Section 1.10.2).

2.2 Recap: Microcontroller Software versus Hardware Description

A C program for a microcontroller (MCU) and a SystemVerilog module may look alike: both have variables, operators, if, and for. Yet they describe fundamentally different things. The C program is a list of instructions that a fixed processor executes one after another. The program counter selects the next instruction, and the time a computation takes follows from the number of executed instructions. A hardware description, in contrast, describes the circuit itself. After synthesis, every line has become gates and flip-flops, and all of them work at the same time, all the time. Time is explicit: the registers update at the clock edge, and the combinational logic between them has one clock period to settle. Table 5 summarizes the differences.

The most important consequence is a change in thinking: think hardware first, then write the code. Before you write a module, sketch its block diagram with the registers and the combinational logic between them (Section 2.2.2). The code then only describes this diagram. Keep in mind that a simulator executes your HDL as an event-driven program on your computer. This is why HDLs also contain constructs such as delays, $display, or file access, which only make sense in simulation and cannot be synthesized (Section 2.5.12).

In the course project (Section 11), you need both ways of thinking. You build a RISC-V SoC: the CPU and the blocks around it are hardware that you describe as registers and combinational logic, and the CPU runs firmware that you write as for any microcontroller. Many SoCs today contain such a processor core, because the firmware keeps the digital part flexible: a new feature or a bug fix in the control flow needs a firmware update instead of a new mask set, while dedicated hardware handles the time-critical parts. RISC-V is a popular choice for this core, since its instruction set is open and can be used and extended without license fees. TinyWhisper (Section 1.12) is an example, with its modem in C firmware on the RISC-V CPU and its IQ modulator in dedicated hardware.

2.2.1 Computing in Time and in Space

The difference between software and hardware becomes obvious with a loop. The following C function counts the ones in a byte (population count):

uint8_t popcount(uint8_t x)
{
  uint8_t count = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 8; i++)
    count += (x >> i) & 1;
  return count;
}

In the loop body, (x >> i) & 1 shifts bit i of x into the least significant position and clears all other bits, so count grows by one exactly when bit i is set. The same function written in SystemVerilog looks almost identical, but the hardware behind it is not a loop at all:

popcount/rtl/popcount.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Combinational population count (number of ones in x_i).

`default_nettype none

module popcount (
  input  logic [7:0] x_i,
  output logic [3:0] count_o
);

  // The loop is unrolled at elaboration: eight adders, no clock cycles
  always_comb begin
    count_o = '0;
    for (int i = 0; i < 8; i++) begin
      count_o = count_o + 4'(x_i[i]);
    end
  end

endmodule

`default_nettype wire

In the loop body, x_i[i] selects bit i directly, without a shift and a mask, and the size cast 4'(...) (Section 2.5.3) extends this 1-bit value explicitly to the 4 bits of count_o, which SystemVerilog would otherwise do silently.

2.2.2 Synchronous Sequential Design

The popcount module is purely combinational: its output depends only on its current inputs. Most circuits also need memory, e.g., for the state of a finite state machine (FSM) or the value of a counter. Almost all of them are built as synchronous sequential circuits with the structure of Figure 4. A register of edge-triggered flip-flops stores the current state. The next-state logic, a purely combinational circuit, computes the next state from the current state and the inputs. At every rising clock edge, the register loads the next state from its D input, and the new state appears at its Q output. The outputs of the circuit are derived from the state (Moore FSM) or from the state and the inputs (Mealy FSM).

Edge-triggered flip-flops are the key to this structure. Their outputs change only at the rising clock edge and stay stable for the rest of the clock period. The next-state logic therefore sees stable inputs for one full clock period, and this is exactly the time it has to compute the next state. The result must have settled before the next rising edge (more precisely, a setup time before it), when the register loads it (Figure 5). The same holds for every path from a register through combinational logic to the next register, so the clock period only has to be longer than the slowest of these paths. Static timing analysis checks exactly this for every path in the design (Section 5). The rule assumes that the inputs also change only after a clock edge, i.e., that they come from registers driven by the same clock. An asynchronous input needs a synchronizer first (Section 9).

In your VHDL designs, you describe this structure with two processes, a register process reg_proc and a combinational process such as next_state_logic. As a minimal example, consider a toggle flip-flop, whose state inverts at every rising clock edge at which toggle_i is high:

-- Register process: only reset and clock in the sensitivity list
reg_proc: process(rst_i, clk_i) is
begin
  if rst_i = '1' then
    state <= '0';
  elsif rising_edge(clk_i) then
    state <= next_state;
  end if;
end process reg_proc;

-- Combinational process: all signals it reads in the sensitivity list
next_state_logic: process(state, toggle_i) is
begin
  -- default assignment
  next_state <= state;

  -- calculate next_state from state and the inputs
  if toggle_i = '1' then
    next_state <= not state;
  end if;
end process next_state_logic;

Three rules make this style work:

  • The register process has only the reset and the clock in its sensitivity list, because the register reacts to nothing else. Its body is always the same: the reset values and, at the rising clock edge, the copies of the next_ signals. No logic belongs here.
  • The sensitivity list of the combinational process contains all signals that are read in the process. The simulator executes the process only when a signal in this list changes, while the synthesis tool ignores the list (at best with a warning) and builds combinational logic anyway. A missing signal therefore makes the simulation differ from the synthesized hardware. VHDL-2008 avoids this problem with process(all).
  • The combinational process starts with a default assignment, here next_state <= state, followed by all decisions. Every signal is thus assigned on every path, and no latch can arise. Keeping the state is not a latch: the register is the memory, and the next-state logic only feeds the current state back to its D input.

2.3 The HDL Landscape

VHDL and Verilog appeared in the 1980s, and for decades they were practically the only choice. In the last fifteen years, a large number of alternatives has appeared, most of them open source. They fall into four families:

  1. Standardized HDLs. VHDL (IEEE 1076), Verilog (IEEE 1364), and SystemVerilog (IEEE 1800, which absorbed Verilog in 2009) are read directly by the commercial simulation, synthesis, and verification tools. Among the open-source tools, (System)Verilog has much broader support than VHDL (Table 8).
  2. Embedded domain-specific languages (eDSLs), also called hardware construction languages. The hardware is described with a library of a general-purpose language such as Scala, Python, or Haskell. Running the program generates the hardware as Verilog, so the classes, functions, loops, and type system of the host language are available to build parameterizable generators.
  3. New standalone HDLs with their own syntax and compiler, designed to avoid the pitfalls of Verilog. They also emit (System)Verilog.
  4. High-level synthesis (HLS) and compiler intermediate representations. An algorithm written in C/C++ or a dataflow language is scheduled into clock cycles by the tool, or a compiler uses an intermediate language as its hardware back end.

2.5.1 Modules, Ports, and Parameters

An unsigned adder with a generic width and a carry bit is a good first example. In VHDL, it needs an entity, an architecture, three type conversions, and a resize:

adder/vhdl/adder_ea.vhd
-- SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
-- SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
-- Description: Unsigned adder with a generic width and a carry bit.

library ieee;
use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;
use ieee.numeric_std.all;

entity adder is
  generic(
    WIDTH : positive := 8
  );
  port(
    a_i   : in  std_logic_vector(WIDTH - 1 downto 0);
    b_i   : in  std_logic_vector(WIDTH - 1 downto 0);
    sum_o : out std_logic_vector(WIDTH downto 0)
  );
end entity adder;

architecture rtl of adder is
begin
  sum_o <= std_logic_vector(resize(unsigned(a_i), WIDTH + 1)
                            + unsigned(b_i));
end architecture rtl;

In SystemVerilog, a single module replaces entity and architecture:

adder/rtl/adder.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Unsigned adder with a parameterized width and a carry bit.

`default_nettype none

module adder #(
  parameter int unsigned WIDTH = 8
) (
  input  logic [WIDTH-1:0] a_i,
  input  logic [WIDTH-1:0] b_i,
  output logic [WIDTH:0]   sum_o
);

  assign sum_o = {1'b0, a_i} + {1'b0, b_i};

endmodule

`default_nettype wire
  • Parameters are declared in the #( ... ) list before the ports, like generics. The type int unsigned makes the parameter a non-negative 32-bit integer. Constants that must not be changed from outside are localparams.
  • Ports are declared in the module header (ANSI style) with direction, type, width, and name. We use the suffixes _i, _o, and _io for inputs, outputs, and bidirectional ports.
  • No type conversions. SystemVerilog has no separate types for bit vectors and numbers, so + works directly on logic vectors. The concatenations {1'b0, a_i} and {1'b0, b_i} make the extension to WIDTH+1 bits explicit. The carry would be kept without them as well, since the target is WIDTH+1 bits wide (Section 2.5.3), but the explicit version states the intent.
  • Case sensitivity. Unlike VHDL, SystemVerilog is case-sensitive: Clk and clk are two different signals. Write signals in lowercase snake_case and parameters in UPPERCASE.
  • `default_nettype none at the top of the file makes an undeclared name an error. Without it, Verilog silently creates a 1-bit wire for a misspelled name on the left side of an assign or in a port connection. The `default_nettype wire at the end restores the default for files compiled afterwards.
  • One module per file, and the file name equals the module name (adder.sv). Pass all files to the tools as a list instead of pulling modules in with `include, so that the list shows every file of the design and no module is read twice. Even in a list, a `define stays active for all files that follow, in every tool. Icarus Verilog also keeps `default_nettype, while Verilator and Yosys reset it for each file. Ending every file with `default_nettype wire gives the same result in all tools.

2.5.2 Data Types and Literals

The everyday type of SystemVerilog is logic. It has four values: 0, 1, x (unknown), and z (high impedance). VHDL’s std_logic has nine values, but the four that matter in RTL map directly. As in VHDL, where an uninitialized std_logic is 'U', a logic that is never assigned or reset is x in a four-state simulator such as Icarus Verilog, so a missing reset stays visible in the waveform. Verilator is a two-state simulator for speed and replaces x with 0 or a random value, which is one reason to simulate with both.

  • Use logic for every signal in RTL. A logic variable assigned in an always_comb block, an always_ff block, or by an assign must not be assigned anywhere else. Verilator reports a second driver as a MULTIDRIVEN warning. Icarus Verilog reports an error only if one of the drivers is an assign, and it accepts two always_comb or two always_ff blocks without a message. The single-driver rule corresponds to the unresolved std_ulogic in VHDL, not to the resolved std_logic, which allows several drivers. Nets with several drivers (tri-state buses) need the type wire and only appear at the pads of an ASIC.
  • wire and reg are legacy Verilog. Despite its name, reg does not mean register. Whether a flip-flop is created depends only on how a variable is assigned, never on its type.
  • Vectors are declared as logic [7:0] data, like std_logic_vector(7 downto 0) with the MSB on the left. Select a bit with data[0], a fixed range with data[7:4], and a range at a variable position with data[i*4 +: 4] (four bits starting at bit i*4).
  • Signedness. Vectors are unsigned by default, logic signed [15:0] acc declares a signed one, and $signed() and $unsigned() convert. If only one operand of an expression is unsigned, the whole expression is evaluated unsigned, so do not mix them.
  • Two-state types such as bit, int (32 bits, signed), and int unsigned know only 0 and 1. Use them for parameters, loop variables, and in testbenches, but not for RTL signals, where they would turn an x into a 0 and hide bugs.
  • Arrays. A packed array logic [3:0][7:0] word is a 32-bit vector made of four bytes and can be used as one vector. An unpacked array logic [7:0] mem [16] holds 16 separate bytes, e.g., a register file.

Literals carry their width in front of the base, as Table 10 shows. Always size your literals, because an unsized number such as 20 is a 32-bit integer.

2.5.3 Operators and Expression Widths

Most operators exist in both languages, but SystemVerilog uses the C notation (Table 11).

The biggest difference from VHDL is how widths are handled. In SystemVerilog, the operands of an expression are extended to the width of the widest operand and of the assignment target, and the result is then truncated to the target. Nothing is reported as an error:

logic [7:0] a, b, sum8;
logic [8:0] sum9;
logic [3:0] nibble, count;
logic       hit;

assign sum8   = a + b;          // carry silently lost, no warning
assign sum9   = a + b;          // carry kept, the target is 9 bits wide
assign nibble = a;              // MSBs cut off (Verilator: WIDTHTRUNC)
assign hit    = (count == 20);  // always false (Verilator: WIDTHEXPAND)

In VHDL, sum9 <= a + b and nibble <= a are length mismatches: GHDL already warns about nibble <= a during the analysis and stops the simulation with a bound check failure. sum8 loses the carry in both languages. Verilator reports the truncation and the comparison with a constant that does not fit, but not the lost carry. So think about the width of every arithmetic result, and make extensions explicit with a concatenation such as {1'b0, a} or a size cast such as 9'(a).

2.5.4 Concurrent Assignments

The VHDL concurrent signal assignment becomes an assign statement, a continuous assignment that is re-evaluated whenever one of its operands changes:

// VHDL: y_o <= a_i and b_i;
assign y_o   = a_i & b_i;

// VHDL: mux_o <= a_i when sel_i = '1' else b_i;
assign mux_o = sel_i ? a_i : b_i;

A selected signal assignment (with ... select) becomes a case statement in an always_comb block (Section 2.5.8). Unlike VHDL-93, SystemVerilog lets you read an output port inside the module, so the internal copy of an output that VHDL needs is not necessary.

2.5.5 Processes: always_comb, always_ff, and always_latch

SystemVerilog has three dedicated process types for synthesizable code, which replace the general-purpose always block of Verilog:

  • always_comb describes combinational logic. It is sensitive to every signal it reads, like process(all) in VHDL-2008, so the sensitivity list can never be incomplete. The tools also check that the block is really combinational: if a variable is not assigned on every path, Verilator warns (LATCH) and Yosys stops with an error.
  • always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) describes flip-flops. Its event list contains only the clock edge and, for an asynchronous reset, the reset edge.
  • always_latch describes an intended latch, which you will rarely need.

Within these blocks, SystemVerilog has two kinds of assignments, which correspond to the variables and signals of VHDL:

  • The blocking assignment = updates the variable immediately, and the next statement already sees the new value, like a VHDL variable (:=).
  • The non-blocking assignment <= evaluates the right-hand side immediately but updates the variable only after all blocks triggered by the same clock edge have read their inputs, like a VHDL signal assignment.

The difference matters as soon as a register reads another register that is updated at the same clock edge. The following two registers swap their values at every clock edge:

always_ff @(posedge clk_i) begin
  a_q <= b_q;
  b_q <= a_q;  // reads the old value of a_q: a real swap
end

With blocking assignments in this block, b_q would read the new value of a_q, and both registers would end up with the old value of b_q, in simulation and in synthesis. It gets worse when the two blocking assignments sit in two separate always_ff blocks. The simulation result then depends on the order in which the simulator runs the blocks, which the standard leaves open, while synthesis builds the swap anyway. Icarus Verilog 14 and Verilator 5.052 both show no swap for this case, while the netlist of Yosys swaps. This mismatch between simulation and synthesis is one of the most common SystemVerilog bugs, and the following rules avoid it.

ImportantThe Golden Rules of Processes
  1. In always_ff, use only non-blocking assignments <=.
  2. In always_comb, use only blocking assignments =.
  3. Assign every variable in exactly one block (or one assign).
  4. Never use the plain always block or initial in RTL, only always_comb, always_ff, and always_latch.

2.5.6 Registers and Resets

In your VHDL style, every register process has an active-high asynchronous reset (Section 2.2.2). In SystemVerilog, the three register variants you need look as follows:

// Asynchronous active-low reset (the default in this course)
always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
  if (!rst_ni) begin
    q <= '0;
  end else begin
    q <= d;
  end
end

// Synchronous active-high reset (counter of the AMS chip template)
always_ff @(posedge clk_i) begin
  if (reset_i) begin
    q <= '0;
  end else begin
    q <= d;
  end
end

// No reset (pure data path registers)
always_ff @(posedge clk_i) begin
  q <= d;
end

In this course, the default is an asynchronous active-low reset named rst_ni (_n for active low, _i for input). This is the common convention in ASIC design and the rule of the lowRISC style guide. It also matches the hardware: the reset flip-flops of the SG13CMOS5L standard-cell library, e.g., sg13cmos5l_dfrbp_1 and sg13cmos5l_dfrbpq_1, have an active-low reset pin RESET_B, and the reset pad of a chip is usually active low (rst_n in the chip core of the AMS chip template). Synthesis handles either polarity: an active-high reset only costs an inverter.

  • The reset test must be the first statement in the block, and its polarity must match the event list (negedge rst_ni with if (!rst_ni)). Otherwise, the block cannot be mapped onto a flip-flop with an asynchronous reset.
  • An asynchronous reset may be asserted at any time, but it must be released synchronously to the clock with a reset synchronizer (Section 9).
  • A synchronous reset is ordinary logic in front of the D input of a flip-flop without reset.
  • A clock enable, written as end else if (en_i) begin q <= d; end, keeps the old value when en_i is low. The synthesis tool maps it onto a flip-flop with an enable input or a multiplexer in front of D. This is a register, not a latch.

2.5.7 The Two-Process Style

In your VHDL designs, you split each block into a register process (reg_proc), which only holds the reset values and copies the next_ signals into the registers, and one or more combinational processes, which start with complete default assignments and contain all decisions (Section 2.2.2). This style maps one to one onto SystemVerilog, and we keep it: always_ff is the register process, always_comb the combinational process, and the VHDL pair next_state and state becomes state_d (the next value at the D input of the register in Figure 4) and state_q (the current value at its Q output), as in the lowRISC style guide. Here is the toggle flip-flop of Section 2.2.2 in SystemVerilog:

// Register process: only clock and reset in the event list
always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
  if (!rst_ni) begin
    state_q <= 1'b0;
  end else begin
    state_q <= state_d;
  end
end

// Combinational process: the sensitivity list is inferred
always_comb begin
  // default assignment
  state_d = state_q;

  // calculate state_d from state_q and the inputs
  if (toggle_i) begin
    state_d = ~state_q;
  end
end

The three rules of the VHDL style carry over. always_comb makes the second one unnecessary, and the tools check the other two:

  • The event list of always_ff contains only the clock edge and the reset edge, just like the sensitivity list of reg_proc. If you add a data signal to it, Yosys stops with an error, even though Verilator accepts it.
  • always_comb infers the complete sensitivity list by itself, so a forgotten signal is no longer possible.
  • The default assignment state_d = state_q; comes first, as in VHDL. If you forget it, the tools report the latch: Verilator warns, and Yosys stops with an error (Section 2.5.8).

The register process uses non-blocking assignments <= and the combinational process blocking assignments =, as the Golden Rules of Section 2.5.5 demand. The order of the two blocks in the file does not matter, since both are concurrent. As in your VHDL designs, all listings of this chapter put the register process first and the combinational process second. Yosys 0.70 synthesizes the toggle flip-flop into exactly two cells, an inverter and a flip-flop with enable ($_DFFE_PN0P_) whose enable input is toggle_i. The default assignment that keeps the state has become the enable of the flip-flop, not a latch.

library ieee;
use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;
use ieee.numeric_std.all;
use ieee.math_real.all;

    rst_i  : in  std_logic;
    en_i   : in  std_logic;
    tick_o : out std_logic

architecture rtl of tick_gen is
  constant C_COUNT_WIDTH : natural := natural(ceil(log2(real(DIV))));

  subtype t_count is unsigned(C_COUNT_WIDTH - 1 downto 0);

  constant C_LAST : t_count := to_unsigned(DIV - 1, C_COUNT_WIDTH);

  signal count      : t_count;
  signal next_count : t_count;
  signal tick       : std_logic;
  signal next_tick  : std_logic;
begin
  -- Register process: reset values and copies of the next_ signals only
  reg_proc: process(rst_i, clk_i) is
  begin
    if rst_i = '1' then
      count <= (others => '0');
      tick <= '0';
    elsif rising_edge(clk_i) then
      count <= next_count;
      tick <= next_tick;
    end if;
  end process reg_proc;

  -- Combinational process: defaults first, then all decisions
  counter_logic: process(count, en_i) is
  begin
    next_count <= count;
    next_tick <= '0';
    if en_i = '1' then
      if count = C_LAST then
        next_count <= (others => '0');
        next_tick <= '1';
      else
        next_count <= count + 1;
      end if;
    end if;
  end process counter_logic;

  tick_o <= tick;
end architecture rtl;

        count_d = '0;
        tick_d  = 1'b1;
      end else begin

      end
    end
  end

  assign tick_o = tick_q;

The two-process style has three advantages. The register process is a fixed template that you never have to think about again. The defaults at the top of the combinational process make latches impossible. And the next values (_d) are signals that you can inspect in the waveform and reuse in other logic. The single-process style, in which the next-state logic is written directly into always_ff (as in the counter of the AMS chip template, Section 2.8), is fine for simple registers and counters, and both styles synthesize to the same hardware. We recommend the two-process style whenever the next-state logic is more than a line or two, and always for FSMs.

2.5.8 if, case, and Latches

if, else if, and else work as if, elsif, and else in VHDL and describe a priority. Unlike in VHDL, a condition does not need to be of type boolean: every nonzero value is true, so you write if (en_i) instead of if en_i = '1' then. The case statement works as in VHDL, with default instead of when others. There are two important differences:

  • VHDL requires a case to cover all values, but SystemVerilog does not. A missing value in an always_comb block leaves the variable unassigned for that value, and the tools infer a latch that holds the old value.
  • The qualifier unique in unique case states that exactly one item matches at a time, so the items can be implemented as a parallel multiplexer instead of a priority chain. Simulators that support it report a violation at run time. Icarus Verilog prints a “sorry” message that it ignores the qualifier, but it still warns at run time when no item matches. It does not check for overlapping items. Use unique case together with a default item.

For decoders, casez treats ? in the case items as don’t care, e.g., 4'b01??. Avoid casex, which also treats an x in the case expression as don’t care and thereby hides x values in simulation.

A latch appears whenever a combinational block does not assign a variable on every path:

// Latch: y_o keeps its old value while en_i is low
always_comb begin
  if (en_i) y_o = a_i;
end

// Fixed: default assignment first, then the decisions
always_comb begin
  y_o = '0;
  if (en_i) y_o = a_i;
end

In a synchronous design, latches are almost always a bug: static timing analysis treats them differently from flip-flops (Section 5), they pass glitches, and they complicate the scan test (Section 8). The SG13CMOS5L library contains latch cells (e.g., sg13cmos5l_dlhq_1), so an unintended latch synthesizes without complaint if nobody checks. Here always_comb pays off: for the plain Verilog block always @(*), Yosys 0.70 only prints a warning and builds the latches, while it stops with an error for always_comb. If you really need a latch, write it with always_latch, so that your intention is documented.

2.5.9 Finite State Machines

FSMs are where the two-process style pays off most. As an example, consider a simple controller: a start_i pulse moves it from idle to a run state, in which busy_o is high, until last_i is high. Then done_o is high for one clock cycle, and the FSM returns to idle. Both outputs depend only on the state (Moore FSM). Figure 7 shows the state diagram, with the outputs written inside the states.

In VHDL, the controller looks as follows:

ctrl_fsm/vhdl/ctrl_fsm_ea.vhd
-- SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
-- SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
-- Description: Moore FSM of a simple controller. A start_i pulse
-- moves it from idle to run (busy_o high) until last_i, then done_o
-- is high for one clock cycle before the FSM returns to idle.

library ieee;
use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;

entity ctrl_fsm is
  port(
    clk_i   : in  std_logic;
    rst_i   : in  std_logic;
    start_i : in  std_logic;
    last_i  : in  std_logic;
    busy_o  : out std_logic;
    done_o  : out std_logic
  );
end entity ctrl_fsm;

architecture rtl of ctrl_fsm is
  type t_state is (CTRL_IDLE, CTRL_RUN, CTRL_DONE);

  signal state      : t_state;
  signal next_state : t_state;
begin
  reg_proc: process(rst_i, clk_i) is
  begin
    if rst_i = '1' then
      state <= CTRL_IDLE;
    elsif rising_edge(clk_i) then
      state <= next_state;
    end if;
  end process reg_proc;

  fsm_logic: process(state, start_i, last_i) is
  begin
    next_state <= state;
    busy_o <= '0';
    done_o <= '0';
    case state is
      when CTRL_IDLE =>
        if start_i = '1' then
          next_state <= CTRL_RUN;
        end if;
      when CTRL_RUN =>
        busy_o <= '1';
        if last_i = '1' then
          next_state <= CTRL_DONE;
        end if;
      when CTRL_DONE =>
        done_o <= '1';
        next_state <= CTRL_IDLE;
    end case;
  end process fsm_logic;
end architecture rtl;

In SystemVerilog:

ctrl_fsm/rtl/ctrl_fsm.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Moore FSM of a simple controller. A start_i pulse
// moves it from idle to run (busy_o high) until last_i, then done_o
// is high for one clock cycle before the FSM returns to idle.

`default_nettype none

module ctrl_fsm (
  input  logic clk_i,
  input  logic rst_ni,
  input  logic start_i,
  input  logic last_i,
  output logic busy_o,
  output logic done_o
);

  typedef enum logic [1:0] {
    StIdle,
    StRun,
    StDone
  } state_e;

  state_e state_d, state_q;

  // State register
  always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
    if (!rst_ni) begin
      state_q <= StIdle;
    end else begin
      state_q <= state_d;
    end
  end

  // Next-state and output logic, defaults first
  always_comb begin
    state_d = state_q;
    busy_o  = 1'b0;
    done_o  = 1'b0;
    unique case (state_q)
      StIdle: begin
        if (start_i) state_d = StRun;
      end
      StRun: begin
        busy_o = 1'b1;
        if (last_i) state_d = StDone;
      end
      StDone: begin
        done_o  = 1'b1;
        state_d = StIdle;
      end
      default: state_d = StIdle;  // unused encoding 2'b11
    endcase
  end

endmodule

`default_nettype wire
  • The state type is a typedef enum with an explicit base type (logic [1:0]). Following the lowRISC style guide, the type name ends in _e and the state names are UpperCamelCase with the prefix St.
  • In VHDL, a case over an enumeration type with three values is complete. The 2-bit state of the SystemVerilog version has a fourth encoding, 2'b11, which the default item sends back to idle.
  • As in VHDL, the next state defaults to the current state, and every output defaults to its inactive value before the case.
  • The synthesis tool is free to choose the state encoding. Yosys 0.70 recognizes this FSM and re-encodes it one-hot, with three flip-flops instead of two.
  • Write the transitions with if and else. A conditional operator between enumeration values, such as state_d = start_i ? StRun : StIdle;, is rejected by Icarus Verilog 14, which requires an explicit cast for it.

An instance of the adder of Section 2.5.1 with a width of 16 bits looks as follows in VHDL:

adder_0: entity work.adder

    WIDTH => 16

logic [3:0][7:0] lane_a, lane_b;

2.5.11 Packages, Typedefs, and Functions

A package collects constants, types, and functions that several modules share, like a VHDL package:

alu_pkg.sv
package alu_pkg;

  localparam int unsigned DATA_WIDTH = 8;

  typedef enum logic [1:0] {
    OpAdd,
    OpSub,
    OpAnd,
    OpOr
  } alu_op_e;

  typedef struct packed {
    alu_op_e               op;
    logic [DATA_WIDTH-1:0] operand;
  } instr_t;

  function automatic logic is_zero(input logic [DATA_WIDTH-1:0] data);
    is_zero = (data == '0);
  endfunction

endpackage

A module refers to the package contents with the scope operator ::, and the package file must be compiled before the modules that use it:

module alu (
  input  logic [alu_pkg::DATA_WIDTH-1:0] a_i,
  input  alu_pkg::instr_t                instr_i,
  output logic [alu_pkg::DATA_WIDTH-1:0] result_o,
  output logic                           zero_o
);

  always_comb begin
    unique case (instr_i.op)
      alu_pkg::OpAdd: result_o = a_i + instr_i.operand;
      alu_pkg::OpSub: result_o = a_i - instr_i.operand;
      alu_pkg::OpAnd: result_o = a_i & instr_i.operand;
      default:        result_o = a_i | instr_i.operand;
    endcase
  end

  assign zero_o = alu_pkg::is_zero(result_o);

endmodule
  • A typedef struct packed replaces a VHDL record. Its fields are accessed with a dot (instr_i.op), and the whole struct is also a plain bit vector, so it can be used as a port or stored in a register.
  • A function automatic describes combinational logic, and every call becomes a separate copy of that logic. Declare functions automatic, so that every call gets its own variables. Tasks, which may contain timing, belong in testbenches.
ImportantTool Support for SystemVerilog Features

The default SystemVerilog front end of Yosys, which LibreLane uses unless USE_SLANG: True is set in its configuration, supports only part of the language. In Yosys 0.70, it fails on three constructs that Verilator and Icarus Verilog accept: import alu_pkg::* (the counterpart of use work.alu_pkg.all), return statements in functions, and unpacked arrays as ports. An import inside the module or its header gives a syntax error, and one before the module keyword gives a syntax error or an internal error. The code of this chapter therefore uses the scope operator pkg::NAME, assigns a function result to the function name, and uses packed arrays at ports. The slang front end of Yosys (read_slang, enabled in LibreLane with USE_SLANG: True) accepts all of these constructs. For the same reason, the counter of the AMS chip template uses `define macros instead of a package. When you use struct fields inside always_comb, Icarus Verilog prints a “sorry” message that the block becomes sensitive to the whole struct, which does not change the result.

Synthesis understands only a subset of SystemVerilog. Everything else is for simulation and testbenches only (Table 14).

2.5.13 Verilog versus SystemVerilog

SystemVerilog grew out of Verilog. Verilog became the IEEE standard 1364 in 1995, was revised in 2001 and 2005, and was merged into SystemVerilog (IEEE 1800) in 2009. SystemVerilog is therefore a superset of Verilog: a Verilog file is also valid SystemVerilog, unless it uses one of the new keywords, such as logic or bit, as a name. In this course, you write SystemVerilog, but you will read plain Verilog all the time:

  • Netlists. Yosys and LibreLane write their netlists in plain Verilog, e.g., final/nl/counter_top.nl.v of the counter macro in the AMS chip template: a port list in the style of Verilog-1995, a long list of wires, and instances of standard cells such as sg13cmos5l_dfrbpq_1.
  • Cell models. A gate-level simulation needs a simulation model of every standard cell. The models in the PDK (sg13cmos5l_stdcell.v, with the user-defined primitives in sg13cmos5l_udp.v) are written in Verilog with gate primitives, user-defined primitives, and specify blocks for the timing.
  • Generated code. All alternative HDLs of Section 2.3 emit Verilog or SystemVerilog.
  • Existing code. Many open-source cores, older IP blocks, textbooks, and online examples are written in Verilog-2001, as is the Verilog cheat-sheet of Section 2.4. AI assistants often suggest code in the same style.

Table 15 maps the Verilog idioms you will meet onto the SystemVerilog of this chapter.

Most of these differences are syntax, but three of them matter beyond syntax:

  • reg and wire follow the assignment, not the hardware. In Verilog, a signal assigned in an always block must be a reg, even if it is combinational, and a signal driven by an assign or by an instance must be a wire. If you turn an assign into an always block, you therefore have to change the declaration as well. In SystemVerilog, a variable (logic or reg) may be assigned in an always block or by a single assign, so the declaration stays the same, and no type name suggests a register that does not exist. Only a wire assigned in an always block remains an error (Icarus Verilog: not a valid l-value, Verilator: PROCASSWIRE for an internal wire).
  • The process types state the intent. For a missing assignment in always @(*), Verilator and Yosys 0.70 only warn, and Yosys builds the latch. For always_comb, Yosys stops with an error (Section 2.5.8).
  • Checks belong in the language, not in comments. In Verilog, the comment // synopsys full_case parallel_case after a case tells the synthesis tool that all missing values are don’t care (full_case) and that the items never overlap (parallel_case). Yosys reads it and builds no latch, and Verilator reads it as well: it drops the LATCH and CASEINCOMPLETE warnings and stops the simulation when no item matches. Icarus Verilog ignores the comment and keeps the old value, so its simulation can differ from the netlist. Write a complete case with a default item instead, and add unique if the items are mutually exclusive. Keep the default with unique as well. Without it, Yosys handles unique like full_case: it silently takes the missing values as don’t care, even in always_comb, and the netlist can differ from the simulation.

Most testbench features of this chapter are SystemVerilog as well, e.g., $fatal, $isunknown(), and int variables, and the verification part of the language (classes, randomization, assertions, and coverage) has no Verilog counterpart.

Finally, the tools must know which language they read. Verilator reads every file as SystemVerilog, also a .v file. Icarus Verilog reads Verilog-2005 unless you select SystemVerilog with -g2012, and the Yosys command read_verilog needs the option -sv, even for a file that ends in .sv. Name your files .sv, so that readers and editors know which language is inside.

The following priority encoder with registered outputs is written in plain Verilog-2001, as you find it in many older designs: idx is the index of the lowest set bit of req, and valid is high if any bit of req is set. Besides its old style, the module contains one mistake.

// Description: Priority encoder with registered outputs in plain
// Verilog-2001. idx is the index of the lowest set bit of req,
// valid is high if any bit of req is set. This file contains a

  input          rst_n;
  input  [N-1:0] req;

TipSolution: Modernize a Verilog-2001 Module
  1. Each plain Verilog construct and its SystemVerilog counterpart:
    • The port list with separate declarations (Verilog-1995 style) becomes an ANSI header with logic ports.
    • The regs (the outputs and idx_next, assigned in always blocks) and the wire valid_next (driven by an assign) all become logic.

    • The untyped parameters become parameter int unsigned, and W, which must be set to match N by hand, becomes $clog2(N).
    • The file extension .v becomes .sv.
  2. Verilator 5.052 reports two warnings. WIDTHTRUNC: the 32-bit integer i is assigned to the 3-bit idx_next. LATCH: if no bit of req is set, the loop assigns nothing, and idx_next keeps its old value. Yosys 0.70 only prints the warning Latch inferred for signal `\prio_enc.\idx_next' and builds 23 cells, among them four flip-flops $_DFF_PN0_ for idx and valid and three latches $_DLATCH_N_ for idx_next. Leaving a value unassigned does not mean “don’t care” but “keep the old value.”
  3. With always_comb, Yosys stops with ERROR: Latch inferred for signal `\prio_enc.\idx_d' from always_comb process. Verilator reports the same LATCH warning as before, and WIDTHTRUNC again unless your translation already casts i.
  4. The fix is a default assignment, as in every combinational process of this chapter:
prio_enc/rtl/prio_enc.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Priority encoder with registered outputs. idx_o
// is the index of the lowest set bit of req_i, valid_o is high if
// any bit of req_i is set.

`default_nettype none

module prio_enc #(
  parameter int unsigned N = 8
) (
  input  logic                 clk_i,
  input  logic                 rst_ni,
  input  logic [N-1:0]         req_i,
  output logic [$clog2(N)-1:0] idx_o,
  output logic                 valid_o
);

  localparam int unsigned W = $clog2(N);

  logic [W-1:0] idx_d, idx_q;
  logic         valid_d, valid_q;

  // Register process: reset values and copies of the _d signals only
  always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
    if (!rst_ni) begin
      idx_q   <= '0;
      valid_q <= 1'b0;
    end else begin
      idx_q   <= idx_d;
      valid_q <= valid_d;
    end
  end

  // Combinational process: defaults first, then all decisions
  always_comb begin
    idx_d   = '0;
    valid_d = |req_i;
    // The loop runs from the highest to the lowest bit, so the
    // lowest set bit is assigned last and wins.
    for (int i = N - 1; i >= 0; i--) begin
      if (req_i[i]) begin
        idx_d = W'(i);
      end
    end
  end

  assign idx_o   = idx_q;
  assign valid_o = valid_q;

endmodule

`default_nettype wire
  • The width W follows from N with $clog2 (available since Verilog-2005), so it can no longer be set wrong.
  • The size cast W'(i) makes the truncation of the loop variable explicit.
  • The loop variable is declared in the loop. It must be a signed int: with int unsigned, the condition i >= 0 is always true. Verilator 5.052 reports a constant comparison (UNSIGNED) and an infinite loop (INFINITELOOP) and then stops with an internal error.

Verilator reports no warning, and Yosys builds 22 cells with the four flip-flops and no latch. While valid_o is low, idx_o is now 0, where the original kept the last index (or x before the first request). This does not matter, since idx_o is only meaningful while valid_o is high. The default idx_d = idx_q; would also be correct: it keeps the last index in the register, and Yosys turns the three idx flip-flops into flip-flops with enable ($_DFFE_PN0P_), again without a latch.

The testbench prio_enc/testbenches/verilog/prio_enc_tb.sv in the code folder applies all 256 values of req and compares the outputs with a reference model, checking idx_o only while valid_o is high. It also runs with your own translation, as long as it has the parameter N and the ports clk_i, rst_ni, req_i, idx_o, and valid_o:

2.6 Testbenches

A testbench applies stimuli to the design under test (DUT) and checks its responses. It is simulation-only code, so it may use the whole language. Make every testbench self-checking: it compares the outputs with the expected values and reports a clear pass or fail, so that you never have to judge a waveform by eye to know whether a design works. Waveforms are for debugging. This section shows a SystemVerilog and a cocotb testbench for the tick generator of Section 2.5.7. Section 4 covers verification with cocotb in depth.

2.6.1 A SystemVerilog Testbench

tick_gen/testbenches/verilog/tick_gen_tb.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Self-checking SystemVerilog testbench for tick_gen.

`timescale 1ns / 1ps

module tick_gen_tb;

  localparam int unsigned DIV           = 5;
  localparam real         CLK_PERIOD_NS = 20.0;  // 50 MHz

  logic clk   = 1'b0;
  logic rst_n = 1'b0;  // reset is active from time 0
  logic en    = 1'b0;
  logic tick;

  // Device under test (DUT)
  tick_gen #(
    .DIV(DIV)
  ) dut (
    .clk_i (clk),
    .rst_ni(rst_n),
    .en_i  (en),
    .tick_o(tick)
  );

  // Clock generation
  always #(CLK_PERIOD_NS / 2) clk = ~clk;

  // Stimulus and checks: inputs change and outputs are sampled on the
  // falling edge, so they never race with the DUT at the rising edge.
  initial begin
    $dumpfile("tick_gen_tb.vcd");
    $dumpvars(0, tick_gen_tb);

    repeat (2) @(negedge clk);
    rst_n = 1'b1;

    // Disabled: no tick at all
    repeat (3 * DIV) begin
      @(negedge clk);
      if (tick !== 1'b0) $fatal(1, "FAIL: tick while disabled");
    end

    // Enabled: exactly one tick every DIV clock cycles
    en = 1'b1;
    for (int cycle = 1; cycle <= 4 * DIV; cycle++) begin
      @(negedge clk);
      if (tick !== (cycle % DIV == 0)) begin
        $fatal(1, "FAIL: cycle %0d, tick = %b", cycle, tick);
      end
    end

    $display("PASS: tick_gen_tb");
    $finish;
  end

endmodule
  • `timescale 1ns / 1ps sets the unit of the delays (#) and the precision of the simulation.
  • The testbench is a module without ports. It instantiates the DUT with a smaller DIV than the default, which keeps the simulation short.
  • always #(CLK_PERIOD_NS / 2) clk = ~clk; generates the clock. Initial values in declarations are fine in a testbench.
  • The stimulus runs in an initial block. Inputs are changed and outputs are checked on the falling clock edge (@(negedge clk)), while the DUT acts on the rising edge. If the testbench and the DUT used the same edge, the result would depend on the order in which the simulator runs the processes, and Icarus Verilog and Verilator choose this order differently.
  • The checks use !==, which also fails if the output is x, and end the simulation with $fatal(1, ...) on the first error. Do not use $error for this: Icarus Verilog only prints the message, reaches $finish, and exits with status 0, so a script or a make target cannot see that the test failed.
  • $dumpfile and $dumpvars write all signals into a VCD waveform file, and $finish ends the simulation.

2.6.2 A cocotb Testbench

cocotb (cocotb developers, n.d.) runs the testbench as Python code next to the simulator. Python then drives and reads the signals of the DUT through the programming interface of the simulator (the Verilog Procedural Interface, VPI, for Verilog simulators).

tick_gen/testbenches/cocotb/tick_gen_tb.py
# SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
# SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
# Description: Self-checking cocotb testbench for tick_gen.

import os
from pathlib import Path

import cocotb
from cocotb.clock import Clock
from cocotb.triggers import ClockCycles, FallingEdge
from cocotb_tools.runner import get_results, get_runner

DIV = 5  # passed to the DUT as parameter in the runner below


async def start_up(dut):
    """Start a 50 MHz clock and reset the DUT for two cycles."""
    cocotb.start_soon(Clock(dut.clk_i, 20, "ns").start())
    dut.rst_ni.value = 0
    dut.en_i.value = 0
    await ClockCycles(dut.clk_i, 2)
    await FallingEdge(dut.clk_i)
    dut.rst_ni.value = 1


@cocotb.test()
async def test_no_tick_when_disabled(dut):
    """tick_o stays low while en_i is low."""
    await start_up(dut)
    for _ in range(3 * DIV):
        await FallingEdge(dut.clk_i)
        assert dut.tick_o.value == 0, "tick while disabled"


@cocotb.test()
async def test_tick_every_div_cycles(dut):
    """tick_o is high for one cycle every DIV cycles."""
    await start_up(dut)
    dut.en_i.value = 1
    for cycle in range(1, 4 * DIV + 1):
        await FallingEdge(dut.clk_i)
        expected = 1 if cycle % DIV == 0 else 0
        assert dut.tick_o.value == expected, \
            f"cycle {cycle}: expected {expected}, got {dut.tick_o.value}"


def tick_gen_runner():
    sim = os.getenv("SIM", "icarus")
    proj_path = Path(__file__).resolve().parent

    runner = get_runner(sim)
    runner.build(
        sources=[proj_path / "../../rtl/tick_gen.sv"],
        hdl_toplevel="tick_gen",
        parameters={"DIV": DIV},
        always=True,
        waves=True,
        timescale=("1ns", "1ps"),
    )
    results_xml = runner.test(
        hdl_toplevel="tick_gen",
        test_module="tick_gen_tb",
        waves=True,
    )

    # A failing test leaves the exit status at 0, so check the results
    num_tests, num_failed = get_results(results_xml)
    if num_failed:
        raise SystemExit(
            f"FAIL: {num_failed} of {num_tests} tests failed")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    tick_gen_runner()
  • Every function decorated with @cocotb.test() is a test. The tests of a file run one after another in the same simulation, so each one starts its own clock and applies the reset.
  • Tests are coroutines (async def), which wait for simulator events with await: RisingEdge(sig), FallingEdge(sig), ClockCycles(sig, n), or Timer(t, "ns"). As in the SystemVerilog testbench, inputs are driven and outputs are checked on the falling edge.
  • dut.en_i.value = 1 writes an input, and dut.tick_o.value reads an output. A write takes effect at the end of the current time step, so reading the same signal right after writing it still returns the old value.
  • cocotb.start_soon(Clock(dut.clk_i, 20, "ns").start()) starts a 50 MHz clock that runs in parallel with the test.
  • A failing Python assert fails the test, and the f-string message explains why.
  • The function tick_gen_runner() follows the idiom of the AMS chip template: the file runs itself. It selects the simulator from the environment variable SIM (Icarus Verilog by default), builds the DUT with the given parameters, and runs the tests. runner.test() returns the path of the result file results.xml, but a failing test does not change the exit status of python3. The last lines therefore check the results with get_results() and exit with an error if any test failed.

2.7.6 A Quick Synthesis Check with Yosys

A simulation shows what a design does, but not which hardware it becomes. A quick synthesis with Yosys answers this in a second:

read_verilog -sv reads the file with the SystemVerilog front end, synth -top tick_gen synthesizes the design with tick_gen as top module onto generic gates, and stat prints the cell statistics at the end of the log. Check that the number of flip-flops ($_DFF_* and $_DFFE_* cells) matches the registers you intended and that there are no latches ($_DLATCH_* cells). The letters of a flip-flop name give the clock edge, the reset polarity, the reset value, and the enable polarity: $_DFFE_PN0P_ is a flip-flop on the positive clock edge (P) with an active-low reset (N) to zero (0) and an active-high enable (P). In the LibreLane flow, Yosys maps these generic cells onto the standard cells of SG13CMOS5L (Section 6).

pwm/rtl/pwm.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: PWM generator. In every period of 2**WIDTH clock
// cycles, pwm_o is high for exactly duty_i cycles (duty_i = 0 keeps
// it low).

`default_nettype none

module pwm #(
  parameter int unsigned WIDTH = 8
) (
  input  logic             clk_i,
  input  logic             rst_ni,
  input  logic [WIDTH-1:0] duty_i,
  output logic             pwm_o
);

  logic [WIDTH-1:0] count_d, count_q;
  logic             pwm_d, pwm_q;

  always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
    if (!rst_ni) begin
      count_q <= '0;
      pwm_q   <= 1'b0;
    end else begin
      count_q <= count_d;
      pwm_q   <= pwm_d;
    end
  end

  always_comb begin
    count_d = count_q + 1'b1;  // wraps from 2**WIDTH - 1 to 0
    pwm_d   = (count_q < duty_i);
  end

  // Registered output: no glitches on the way to a pad
  assign pwm_o = pwm_q;

endmodule

`default_nettype wire

Since the counter has exactly WIDTH bits, it wraps around without any compare logic. The output register delays pwm_o by one cycle with respect to the comparison, which does not change the number of high cycles per period.

The following VHDL FSM detects the bit pattern 1011 in a serial input. It samples one bit at each rising clock edge, and overlapping occurrences count, so 1011011 contains the pattern twice. detect_o is high for one clock cycle after the edge that sampled the last 1 of the pattern (Moore output).

-- SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
-- SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
-- Description: Moore FSM that detects the bit pattern 1011 in the
-- serial input bit_i (one bit per rising clock edge, overlapping
-- occurrences count). detect_o is high for one cycle after the edge
-- that sampled the last 1.

library ieee;
use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;

entity seq_detect is
  port(
    clk_i    : in  std_logic;
    rst_i    : in  std_logic;
    bit_i    : in  std_logic;
    detect_o : out std_logic
  );
end entity seq_detect;

architecture rtl of seq_detect is
  -- Each state names the longest matched prefix of 1011
  type t_state is (DET_IDLE, DET_1, DET_10, DET_101, DET_1011);

  signal state      : t_state;
  signal next_state : t_state;
begin
  reg_proc: process(rst_i, clk_i) is
  begin
    if rst_i = '1' then
      state <= DET_IDLE;
    elsif rising_edge(clk_i) then
      state <= next_state;
    end if;
  end process reg_proc;

  fsm_logic: process(state, bit_i) is
  begin
    next_state <= state;
    detect_o <= '0';
    case state is
      when DET_IDLE =>
        if bit_i = '1' then
          next_state <= DET_1;
        end if;
      when DET_1 =>
        if bit_i = '0' then
          next_state <= DET_10;
        end if;
      when DET_10 =>
        if bit_i = '1' then
          next_state <= DET_101;
        else
          next_state <= DET_IDLE;
        end if;
      when DET_101 =>
        if bit_i = '1' then
          next_state <= DET_1011;
        else
          next_state <= DET_10;
        end if;
      when DET_1011 =>
        detect_o <= '1';
        if bit_i = '1' then
          next_state <= DET_1;
        else
          next_state <= DET_10;
        end if;
    end case;
  end process fsm_logic;
end architecture rtl;

Each state stores the longest prefix of 1011 that the received bits end with. A 0 after 1011 leaves 10, and a 1 leaves 1, which is how overlapping occurrences are detected. seq_detect/rtl/seq_detect.sv
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0
// Description: Moore FSM that detects the bit pattern 1011 in the
// serial input bit_i (one bit per rising clock edge, overlapping
// occurrences count). detect_o is high for one cycle after the edge
// that sampled the last 1.

`default_nettype none

module seq_detect (
  input  logic clk_i,
  input  logic rst_ni,
  input  logic bit_i,
  output logic detect_o
);

  // Each state names the longest matched prefix of 1011
  typedef enum logic [2:0] {
    StIdle,
    St1,
    St10,
    St101,
    St1011
  } state_e;

  state_e state_d, state_q;

  always_ff @(posedge clk_i or negedge rst_ni) begin
    if (!rst_ni) begin
      state_q <= StIdle;
    end else begin
      state_q <= state_d;
    end
  end

  always_comb begin
    state_d  = state_q;
    detect_o = 1'b0;
    unique case (state_q)
      StIdle: begin
        if (bit_i) state_d = St1;
      end
      St1: begin
        if (!bit_i) state_d = St10;
      end
      St10: begin
        if (bit_i) state_d = St101;
        else       state_d = StIdle;
      end
      St101: begin
        if (bit_i) state_d = St1011;
        else       state_d = St10;
      end
      St1011: begin
        detect_o = 1'b1;
        if (bit_i) state_d = St1;
        else       state_d = St10;
      end
      default: state_d = StIdle;  // unused encodings
    endcase
  end

endmodule

TipSolution: The Counter in the Two-Process Style

Figure 9 shows the counter in the structure of Figure 4. The state is count_q, held in CTR_BW flip-flops with the clock clock_i and the synchronous reset reset_i. The next-state logic gets count_q and the input enable_i and needs four parts: a comparator with CTR_MAX, an incrementer, a first multiplexer that selects zero at the wrap-around, and a second multiplexer that keeps count_q while enable_i is low.

In the code, the register becomes always_ff, and the next-state logic becomes always_comb, where the two multiplexers turn into the if and else decisions:

macros/counter/rtl/counter.sv (two-process version)
// SPDX-FileCopyrightText: 2026 Simon Dorrer and Harald Pretl
// SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0 WITH SHL-2.1
// Description: This file implements an N-bit up counter with
// synchronous reset & enable in SystemVerilog (two-process style).

`default_nettype none
`ifndef __COUNTER__
`define __COUNTER__

module counter #(
  parameter int unsigned       CTR_BW  = 8,
  parameter logic [CTR_BW-1:0] CTR_MAX = 2**CTR_BW - 1
)(
  input logic               clock_i,
  input logic               reset_i,
  input logic               enable_i,

  output logic [CTR_BW-1:0] counter_value_o
);

  logic [CTR_BW-1:0] count_d, count_q;

  // Register process: synchronous reset and copy of count_d only
  always_ff @(posedge clock_i) begin
    if (reset_i) begin
      count_q <= '0;
    end else begin
      count_q <= count_d;
    end
  end

  // Combinational process: next counter value, wraps around at CTR_MAX
  always_comb begin
    count_d = count_q;
    if (enable_i) begin
      if (count_q == CTR_MAX) begin
        count_d = '0;
      end else begin
        count_d = count_q + 1'b1;
      end
    end
  end

  assign counter_value_o = count_q;

endmodule // counter

`endif
`default_nettype wire

2.9 HDL Coding Guidelines

“A consistent naming and schematic drawing style, as well as VHDL/Verilog coding scheme, is a huge help in avoiding errors and increasing productivity.” (Circuit Designer’s Etiquette)

The VHDL/Verilog Coding Guide of the Circuit Designer’s Etiquette was written for Verilog and applies to SystemVerilog with small adaptations. Its rules, restated for the SystemVerilog of this course:

  1. Lint every file with verilator --lint-only -Wall and fix every warning. A warning you ignore today hides the one that matters tomorrow.
  2. Write readable code with descriptive names and comments that explain the intention. Inputs end in _i, outputs in _o, bidirectional ports in _io. An active-low signal has an n before the direction suffix, e.g., rst_ni.
  3. Choose the reset deliberately. A synchronous reset avoids reset races. An asynchronous reset, our default, must be free of glitches and released synchronously (Section 9).
  4. Use logic for all signals. The suffixes _w and _r of plain Verilog for wire and reg are not needed.
  5. Use assign for simple logic. The conditional operator c ? a : b is often clearer than a nested if.
  6. Declare every port explicitly with direction, type, and width (output logic [7:0] data_o).
  7. Use localparam for constants and write parameters in UPPERCASE.
  8. Reset every register that holds control state, e.g., FSM states, counters, and valid flags, to a defined value.
  9. One module per file, and the file name equals the module name.
  10. Start every file with `default_nettype none and end it with `default_nettype wire, as a safety net against typos.
  11. Use always_comb for combinational logic. It replaces the always @(*) of plain Verilog and never has an incomplete sensitivity list.
  12. Parameterize bit widths and other values with parameters and localparams.
  13. Make conversions and widths explicit instead of relying on implicit extension and truncation (Section 2.5.3).
  14. Use blocking assignments = only in always_comb and non-blocking assignments <= only in always_ff.
  15. Make visible whether a control acts synchronously or asynchronously. The etiquette names synchronous set and reset set and res, and asynchronous ones pre (preset) and clr (clear). In this course, the asynchronous reset of a block is rst_ni, and a synchronous reset or clear is an ordinary input, such as reset_i in the counter of the AMS chip template.

From the lowRISC style guide (lowRISC, n.d.), we additionally adopt the suffixes _d and _q for the next and current value of a register, _e for enumeration types and _t for other types, UpperCamelCase state names, unique case with a default, named connections for every port and parameter, and no plain always blocks. For parameters, however, we keep the UPPERCASE names of the etiquette (rule 7), while lowRISC uses UpperCamelCase. Finally, Table 20 collects the traps that VHDL designers fall into most often.

2.10 Summary

  • An HDL describes hardware that works concurrently, not instructions that run one after another. Think hardware first, then write the code.
  • Almost every digital circuit is a register plus combinational next-state logic. The register loads the next state at every rising clock edge, so the logic has one clock period to compute it.
  • Many HDLs exist, but all alternatives can generate (System)Verilog, which then runs through the same tools. SystemVerilog is the industry standard and the language of our open-source flow.
  • Your VHDL two-process style maps directly onto SystemVerilog: always_ff holds the reset values and copies _d into _q, and always_comb starts with defaults and contains all decisions.
  • Use logic, always_comb with =, always_ff with <=, sized literals, and an asynchronous active-low reset rst_ni. Lint every file and check it with a quick Yosys synthesis.
  • Plain Verilog is everywhere, in netlists, cell models, generated code, and older IP. Read it, but write SystemVerilog: logic instead of reg and wire, always_comb and always_ff instead of always.
  • Make every testbench self-checking, drive and check on the falling clock edge, and make sure that a failure sets the exit status: $fatal in SystemVerilog, get_results() in a cocotb runner.
  • The make targets of the AMS chip template wrap the commands you typed in this chapter, with small differences, e.g., -DSIM and no -Wall (Table 19).
